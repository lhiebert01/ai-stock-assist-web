THE POST:

Right now, some of the strongest businesses in the world look cash-poor on paper. Here's why that's a trap.

Free cash flow is fundamental analysis's lie detector — earnings can be shaped, but cash either arrived or it didn't. The catch: free cash flow is operating cash minus capital spending. A company investing heavily in its future shows the same thin FCF as a company in slow decline. In the middle of an AI-infrastructure build-out, that confusion is everywhere.

So every AI Stock Assist card now carries one sentence in its cash-flow panel:

Bridge: OCF $9.5B − capex $3.8B = FCF $5.7B · capex is 40% of OCF

That last clause is the tell. Capex at 40% of operating cash is a railroad maintaining its network. Above 100% is a bet-the-decade build-out. Neither is automatically good or bad — what matters is that you can SEE which one you're looking at, right next to the verdict it affects.

Companion fix: dividing price by NEGATIVE free cash flow produces a number that looks meaningful and isn't. Our cards print "n/m — not meaningful" with the reason, and the narrative is banned from calling anything "cheapest on cash" unless the cash is positive.

The framework scores today's cash reality. The bridge shows you whether that reality is a choice. What you do with a company that's choosing to look poor — that's judgment, and it's yours.

The card does the arithmetic; you make the decision.
