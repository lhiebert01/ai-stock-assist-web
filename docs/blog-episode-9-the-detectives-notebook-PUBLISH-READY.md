Series: Learning to Invest in the Intelligence Era

Tom has picked stocks "by feel" for ten years, and he's sure he's good at it. Then Alex asks him one uncomfortable question — how do you know? — and pulls up the one thing Tom has never once kept: a record of his own decisions.

The Story So Far…

Our friends have learned to spot opportunity, separate investing from gambling, trust cash over earnings, see past a cheap-looking share price, catch dishonest accounting, dodge a yield trap, read two expert maps that disagree, tell a spreadsheet apart from a judgment — and last week, that the most expensive thing you can lose is the memory of why you decided.

Today, Tom learns a quieter lesson: a decision you never write down is a decision you can never learn from. Ten years of going "by feel" isn't ten years of experience. It's the same year, ten times.

I Just Know

Tom had been listening to the others for weeks, arms folded, amused. Today he finally spoke up.

"I'll be honest with you," he said. "I've been buying stocks for ten years. I don't do all this." He waved a hand at Marcus's notes. "I read about a company, I look at the numbers that day, and I get a feel for it. Good business, fair price — I buy. I've done fine."

"I believe you," Alex said. "Let me ask you one thing, though. The last stock you sold at a loss — why did you buy it in the first place?"

Tom opened his mouth. "It was… a tech thing. I liked the management."

"And the ROE the day you bought it?"

"The what?"

"Return on equity. How much profit the company squeezed out of every dollar shareholders put in. Was it a high-quality business, or just a good story?"

Tom shrugged. "I'd have to look it up. I don't keep all that."

"That's the thing," Alex said. "You don't keep any of it. So here's the real question — and I'm not being unkind." He leaned in. "After ten years of going by feel… how do you actually know your feel is any good?"

Tom started to answer. Then didn't.

"Can I tell you about two detectives?"

————————————————————————

The Detective's Notebook

"Two detectives work the same city," Alex began. "The first one is famous for his instinct. He walks into a room, looks around, and just knows. No notes. 'I've solved a hundred cases,' he says. 'I don't need to write things down — I can feel when something's wrong.'"

"The second one is quieter. After every case she opens a leather notebook and writes it all down. The date. The clues she saw. What she concluded — and why. Then she closes it. And here's the strange part: months later, she reads her own old cases back."

Marcus frowned. "Why? They're solved."

"Because she's not checking the criminal," Alex said. "She's checking herself. She looks at the cases she got right and the ones she got wrong, and she asks: which clues actually predicted the answer — and which ones just felt important? Year after year, her notebook makes her sharper. She learns which of her hunches are real and which are noise."

"And the first detective?"

"He stays exactly as good as he was on day one. He solves the easy ones, misses the hard ones, and never knows the difference — because he kept no record to learn from. He doesn't have ten years of experience." Alex paused. "He has one year of experience, ten times over."

Tom sat very still.

"I'm the first detective."

He tapped the word.

"That word is the whole game, Tom. One high ROE is a story. A high ROE every year is a quality business. And the only way you ever see the difference… is if you keep the record. Going by feel, you'd have looked at one good number and called it great. The notebook is what turns a snapshot into a pattern."

————————————————————————

The Objection

Tom pushed back. "But I do check the numbers. I look them up the day I buy. Why do I need a history? I need today's data, not last year's."

"Today's data tells you about the company," Alex said. "It tells you nothing about you. Whether your judgment is any good. Whether the businesses you're drawn to actually hold up. You can only learn that by comparing what you concluded then to what happened since — and that requires a then. A dated record."

He gestured at the History tab.

"Picking by feel for ten years didn't give you ten years of data, Tom. It gave you ten years of decisions you can't audit. The current numbers tell you where a company stands. Your own notebook is the only thing that tells you whether to trust the person reading them."

• Running an analysis — and saving it, dated, to your casebook automatically: about 25¢ — 30 seconds — and your first three analyses are free
• Tom's record from the last ten years of "feel": $0 — because he never kept one
• What the missing notebook may have cost: drifting roughly 4% a year behind a simple index fund without ever seeing it happen — on a $200,000 portfolio, that's around $80,000 of growth quietly given up over a decade. The pattern was there by year two. He just had no record to read it in. (Illustrative.)

Penny-wise. Pound-foolish.

Tom didn't fall behind because he was reckless. He fell behind because he was confident — and confidence with no record is just a feeling that's never been checked. The notebook costs about a quarter a page, and it's the only thing that ever turns a hunch into a skill.

————————————————————————

The Lesson

Tom turned the laptop back toward Alex, slower now.

"The uncomfortable part," he said, "is that I always thought the not-writing-it-down was the confident move. Like keeping notes was for people who weren't sure."

"It's the opposite," Alex said. "The amateur trusts his memory because checking it might prove him wrong. The professional keeps the notebook because she wants to be proven wrong — early, cheaply, on paper, instead of late and expensively in her account. You don't have to be a genius, Tom. You just have to be willing to read your own old cases."

He slid the laptop across.

"Run one. Today becomes case number one."

Tom looked at the empty History tab for a moment. Then he started typing a ticker.

Metric Spotlight — From the AI Stock Assist Metrics Guide

This episode highlights ROE (Return on Equity) — how much profit a company generates on the money shareholders have put in. It's the single fastest read on business quality, and the number a one-good-quarter story most often flatters.

• Above 20% — Excellent: a highly efficient, high-quality business
• 15–20% — Good: clears Buffett's bar of a consistent 15%+
• Below 10% — Weak: the business isn't using shareholders' money efficiently

Formula: (Net Income / Shareholders' Equity) × 100. Two cautions the notebook is built for: (1) one high ROE is a story — what you want is a high ROE every year, which you only see by keeping the record; (2) heavy debt can inflate ROE artificially, so read it next to the Balance Sheet Health score. See the Metrics Guide for how to read every metric in context.
